Fundamentals of Convolutional Neural Networks
The advent and implementation of computer vision applications is one of the most significant advances in computer paradigms in human history due to its revolutionizing impact in a variety of critical sectors and ubiquitous presence in the modern world. Throughout this exposition, we will draw many details from the work of Lecun et. al and develop the underlying framework for an MNIST trained CNN from the insights into the CNN strucutures presented in the paper 726791. CNNs represent a profound migration away from tranditional MLPs: whereas MLPS require vectorized data that overlooks the rich subtleties of grouped data (as is present in image data), CNNs perform operations that intend to preserve associativity among neighboring regions of data, which lends to it's precedence over other techniques in image recognition tasks. It is also serendipitous that these techniques are well suited to parallelization and vectorization. When desciribing the core advantages of CNNs, we must first investigate the exploitable traits of images: hierarchy, locality, equivariance, and invariance.
1.1
Locality, Feature Selection, and Kernels
CNNs implement the concept of locality via defining structure within a "receptive-field", which is a small, local, region of 2D space (at least in the image paradigm), that is screened with a particular window of weights, referred to as a kernel. Kernels applied to images rely on the concept of translational invariance, such that relevant features in the image will remain consistent despite their location in the image. This allows for significant parameter reudction (in many cases, reducing it to a square root of the total number of parameters that would be needed in a translation variant, or location dependent, circumstance). We may further reduce the number of parameters by localizing on a specific region within 2D space, ignoring the bulk of data outside of the window region. This further reduces the number of parameters to train over, but we must now compensate by implementing an architecture that sequentially evaluates a highly local feature map (image patch + kernel transform output) and passes data to kernels that evaluate larger subspaces of the image to obtain more specificity to the training objective The mathematical exposition below shows the utility of implementing a localizing CNN over raw MLP that trains on translationally invariant data zhang2023dive.
1.0 Raw MLP with Translationally Variant Data

$$ [\mathbf{H}]_{i,j} = [\mathbf{U}]_{i,j} + \sum_k \sum_l [\mathbf{W}]_{i,j,k,l} [\mathbf{X}]_{k,l} \\ $$
In the previous equations, the traditional MLP is recharacterized to represent the aforementioned tenets of image data, and we observe that the final equation (4) renders out a unique system that relies on far fewer trainable parameters than the previous model. This equation encapsulates the shifting window functionality endemic to CNNS, which is effective in reducing the parameter count of the training architecture as well as respecting the local topology present throughout the image, which affords correlated data that is salient to the effective learning of the CNN.
1.1 Raw MLP with Translationally Invariant Data Assumption

$$ [\mathbf{H}]_{i,j} = u + \sum_a \sum_b [\mathbf{V}]_{a,b} [\mathbf{X}]_{i+a,j+b}. $$ 1.2 Locality Restriction (\(\Delta\) is the kernel dimension boundary)
$$ [\mathbf{H}]_{i,j} = u + \sum_{a=-\Delta}^{\Delta} \sum_{b=-\Delta}^{\Delta} [\mathbf{V}]_{a,b} [\mathbf{X}]_{i+a,j+b}. $$ 1.3 Convolution with Feature Summation (c: # of channels)

$$ [\mathbf{H}]_{i,j,d} = \sum_{a=-\Delta}^{\Delta} \sum_{b=-\Delta}^{\Delta} \sum_{c} [\mathbf{V}]_{a,b,c,d} [\mathbf{X}]_{i+a,j+b,c}. $$
1.2
CNN Design Protocols
The nomeclature regarding "convolution" in the design of CNNs is a reference to the conversion of an image data into a feature map via a sliding operation of the kernel. The units (hardcoded weights) in a kernal are varied between kernels that seek to identify different information features, and kernel weights may be a combination of hardcoded weight parameters, and malleable, trainable weights that are learned from the data. The ouput of a kernel referred to as a feature map, and a single convolutional layer may produce several maps with respect to convolutions of the image over different kernel operations (edge detection, noise reduction, cropping, etc.). Because precise position is not only irrelevant in the context of image recognitiion, but potentially hazardous due to variances in the position of data represented by the same class, CNNS utilize a blurring operation, referred to as subsampling, that performs pooling of the "general" signal values of the images (max pooling or pooling averages are an example of this). Subsampling offers the benefit of allowing discretion of classes despite geometric transformations provided that the number of feature maps be set accordingly 726791.
Cross Correlation Operation
The cross-correlation operation (applying a kernel and matrix multiplication), not to be confused with convolution, which is essentially the same procedure with the exception that the kernel is flipped prior to performing the multiplication operation.
Recall that kernels can be hardcoded, such as the finite difference operator, which is a simple kernel that does bounds checking, of the form [1,-1]; kernels are also learnable features (simply a structured collection of weights in 2D space with a bias), and CNNs can be designed to output learned kernel weights after training.

CNN Scaling (EfficientNet)

                    

                  

                  

                    A prominent challenge arises when attempting to scale CNNs to capture more features (by generating more "live-kernels" and yielding more feature maps) or when managing high resolution image data.The research conducted by pmlr-v97-tan19a presents an investigation effort supported by GoogleAI that revolutionized the scalability of CNNs. Traditionally, CNNs are improved by scaling up the size of their architecture, either by depth (the number of convolutional layers), or width (the number of feature extracting kernels that conform to specific learned data, which produce "channels", or different representations of the input based on the parametrization of a specific kernel). The resolution is a metric that typically describes the number of data points contained within an input image sample, but it's meaning extends to the level of granularity that is afforded to CNNs during subsampling phases. The complication is that scaling a specific dimension of a CNN does not generally afford large accuracy improvements as the benefits tend to saturate around 80%. The EfficientNet protocol essentially permutes a combination of depth, width, and resolution paraters, (\(\alpha, \beta, \gamma \)) on a small representative CNN sample and iteratively tests it's performance (this is the "small grid search" referenced in the paper). The search space of the 3 parameters is generally limited and reviewed under the conditions of a constraint condition, which typically manifests as limitations to the availability of computing power (FLOPS). The constraint in the paper is established such that the number of FLOPS scales exponentially by 2. From previosuly supplied data, the research team defined a constraint to limit the growth of the computational requirements by a factor of two respective to the scaling coefficient (for every unit of scale-up, the computational demand doubles), and the constraint is defined as an equation in terms of the three potentially expandable elements of the network. Utilizing this "smart-expansion" technique allows for greater optimizatin of CNN architecture to maximize accuracy gains while respecting constraints to generate a consistent scale up cost for the system.
